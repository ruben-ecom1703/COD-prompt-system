## Ad 5 — Spirit Airlines / Airport Gate

**Hook:** "Two weeks ago I was at the airport when Spirit Airlines shut down overnight..."
**POV:** 1ère personne — père de famille, 42 ans, Charlotte NC
**Persona insider:** Homme en costume, 2 téléphones, badge/ID gouvernement, briefé PAR les agents (pas l'inverse)
**Trigger:** Fermeture brutale Spirit Airlines (17,000 emplois) = premier domino visible
**Mécanisme unique:** L'insider ne cherche PAS le narrateur — c'est le narrateur qui entend un appel et ose poser la question. Le boarding pass Spirit (devenu inutile) sert de support pour l'URL.
**Cascade:** Hormuz fermé → fuel doublé → airlines tombent (Spirit, Lufthansa -20K, KLM, Ryanair) → même fuel = électricité → grid → pompes → eau
**Structure spéciale:** 
- L'insider MONTRE une photo de son tiroir plein de filtres + voiture = preuve visuelle
- La femme du narrateur pose LA question ("Is there a creek near us?") = moment de conversion
- Le narrateur va TESTER au ruisseau seul = démonstration auto-convaincante
- Progression sociale : femme commande 10, frère 8 (après avoir dit "conspiracy theorist"), mère reçoit 4 par courrier
- "Two per person. Don't wait." = refrain + règle mémorisable
- Le boarding pass Spirit comme objet narratif (URL écrite dessus au stylo)

Two weeks ago I was at the airport when Spirit Airlines shut down overnight — every screen in the terminal flipped red, gates went dark, seventeen thousand people lost their jobs before most of them woke up. While everyone rushed the counter trying to rebook on airlines that are quietly cancelling thousands of flights of their own, the man three seats to my right didn't move. Suit that cost more than my rent. Two phones. The kind of guy who looks like he just came from a room where decisions get made about things you'll read about two weeks later. He picked up his phone, dialed one number, and said "cancel everything this month. All of it. I was right." I should have rebooked my flight and gone home. Instead I sat down next to him. What he told me over the next forty minutes in that empty gate is the reason I never rebooked, cancelled my son's summer camp, pulled my daughter out of her July trip, and have spent every night after my kids go to bed doing things my wife thought were crazy until she asked me one question and stopped asking anything at all.



I need to back up.



I'm 42. Wife, two kids — Lily's 9, Mason's 6. I live outside Charlotte. I was flying Spirit to see my mom in Fort Lauderdale because it was $138 round trip and I don't spend money I don't have to.



I got to the airport at 5:30 AM. Coffee from the kiosk. Gate B14. Scrolling my phone like everyone else.



That's when I noticed him.



Not because he was loud. Because he was the quietest person in the building. Three seats away, legs crossed, reading something on his phone the way someone reads something they've been expecting. Dark suit that wasn't off a rack. Leather case on the seat next to him. Two phones. No carry-on. No neck pillow. He didn't look like a man going on a trip. He looked like a man coming back from one.



I remember thinking — lawyer. Or lobbyist. Or something where you fly somewhere, say things behind closed doors, and fly home before anyone knows you were there.



I went back to my phone.



Then the screens went red.



All of them. At the same time. Every board. Every monitor in Terminal A flipped to the same message. "We regret to inform you that Spirit Airlines has ceased all operations effective immediately."



Three seconds of silence. Then chaos. A woman dropped her bag. A family with three kids just froze. A guy in a Panthers hoodie started yelling at the counter agent who looked just as confused as the rest of us. People pushing forward, holding up phones, screaming questions nobody had answers to.



I got up and walked toward the counter. Not to rebook — I didn't even know what was happening yet. None of us did. It hadn't hit the news. No push alerts. Just red screens and a gate agent trying to get fifty people to stop yelling and form a line.



They weren't answering anything. Just repeating the same line — "please remain calm, we'll have more information shortly, please form a single line" — over and over while people shouted over each other.



Then the man in the suit stood up.



He didn't get in line. He walked straight to the counter — past the line, past the guy in the Panthers hoodie who was mid-sentence — and did something I almost missed. He opened the left side of his jacket, just slightly, and showed something to the agent. A card. An ID. Something clipped to the inside pocket.



She looked at it. Squinted. Then her whole face changed.



She didn't tell him to get in line. She picked up a phone behind the counter and called someone. Within thirty seconds another agent came out from a back door I didn't even know was there. The two of them stepped to the side with him — away from the crowd — and started talking. Low. Close.



But here's the thing. They weren't telling him what was going on. He wasn't asking questions. He was standing there, arms crossed, checking his watch, like he was waiting for someone above them to show up. Like THEY were briefing HIM.



The whole thing lasted maybe two minutes. Then he walked back to his seat, sat down, picked up his phone, and made the call.



"Cancel everything this month. All of it. I was right."



He hung up. Put the phone down. Took off his glasses. And just stared out the window at the tarmac like a man watching the first crack spread across a windshield.



I should have sat back down and minded my own business.



But I was only three seats away. And the terminal had gone quiet around us — most people had moved to the counter or left the gate entirely. So when he made his second call, I heard more than I was supposed to.



Not all of it. Fragments. The kind you piece together after.



"...just like we talked about. It's starting."



Then a pause. He was listening to whoever was on the other end.



"...no. Get them ready now. Before the lockdowns start. Not next week. Now."



Another pause.



"...I told you. 2020 was the rehearsal. This is the real one."



He hung up. Sat back. Rubbed his eyes with his thumb and forefinger like a man who hadn't slept in days.



I sat there trying to make sense of what I'd just heard. Lockdowns. Rehearsal. Get them ready. My pulse was doing something it doesn't usually do at six in the morning in an airport.



And then I did something stupid.



I looked at him and said — almost under my breath — "what do you mean 2020 was a rehearsal?"



His head snapped toward me. Not slow. Fast. The way someone reacts when they hear a sound that shouldn't be there.



"What did you just say."



"I — I heard you on the phone. I wasn't trying to listen. But you said—"



"Keep your voice down." He leaned toward me. Not threatening. Controlled. "Do not repeat that. Not here. Not to anyone."



I sat there frozen.



He looked around the gate. Checked who was nearby. The janitor. A woman asleep two rows over. Nobody close.



Then he looked at me like he was running some calculation — risk, trust, whether I was worth the trouble.



"How old are your kids?"



Nine and six.



He took a breath. Looked at the departure board one more time. Then back at me.



"Alright. I'm going to tell you something. And I need you to actually hear it."



He didn't start with some big speech. He just sat there for a second, looking out at the tarmac, and said:



"What do you think just happened here?"



I said what everyone was saying. Spirit went bankrupt. Bad management. Cheap airline finally ran out of money.



"That's what they'll put on the news tonight."



He let that sit.



"You know what actually killed this airline?"



No.



"Fuel. Jet fuel has doubled since March. Doubled. You know what that means for an airline like Spirit — thin margins, cheap tickets, no business class to absorb the hit? Every seat on every plane loses money. So they stopped flying."



I told him okay, fuel prices are up, everyone knows that. But that's Spirit. One budget airline.



"One airline."



"Yeah."



"Lufthansa — one of the biggest airlines on the planet — just cancelled twenty thousand flights through October. Not weather. Not strikes. Fuel."



I didn't say anything.



"KLM cut flights. Vietnam Airlines slashed a quarter of their routes. Cathay Pacific is cutting. Ryanair's CEO said if the strait doesn't open they'll cancel up to ten percent of every flight this summer."



"What strait."



He looked at me like he expected that question.



"The Strait of Hormuz. Twenty-one miles wide. Between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula. Twenty percent of the world's oil moves through that lane every single day. When the war started and Iran closed it — the spigot shut off."



He said it the way you'd explain something to someone who should already know. Not mean. Just tired.



"The head of the International Energy Agency — not a blogger, not a pundit, the top energy authority on the planet — said on the record that Europe has maybe six weeks of jet fuel left. The EU's energy commissioner said this could last months. Maybe years."



Six weeks.



"Spirit's not the story. Spirit is the first domino."



I sat there for a minute. The noise around us — yelling, crying, the gate agent still asking people to please form a line — it sounded different now. Like I was hearing it from underwater.



"First domino of what."



He didn't answer right away. He picked up his phone. Checked something. Put it back down.



"You know where this ends if the strait doesn't open?"



"No."



"Think about what's happening right now. Not here. Everywhere else."



He started listing things the way someone reads off a briefing.



"The Philippines declared a national energy emergency in March. They import 98% of their oil from the Gulf. When the strait shut, their fuel prices doubled overnight. The government put workers on a four-day week to keep people off the roads."



"Sri Lanka made every Wednesday a public holiday. Not a perk. Because they can't afford to let people drive five days a week. They're rationing gas — less than four gallons per person per week. Their president went on television and said 'prepare for the worst.'"



"Bangladesh cut work hours. Shut malls by six. Egypt is dimming streetlights and closing restaurants by nine at night. India ran out of cooking gas. People are burning wood and coal. Thailand is telling citizens to turn off the AC and work from home."



He looked at me.



"That's not one country having a bad month. That's a dozen countries on three continents all doing the same thing at the same time. Cutting hours. Rationing fuel. Telling people to use less. And every one of them is calling it something soft. 'Energy conservation.' 'Temporary measures.' 'Emergency protocols.'"



"And you're saying it's not temporary."



"I'm saying it's the first round."



He leaned forward.



"Now think about where the flights are going. Spirit's dead. Lufthansa cut twenty thousand. Vietnam Airlines gutted a quarter of their routes. Jet fuel doubled. Think two years out. Three years. If this doesn't stop — and there is no reason right now to believe it stops — who is still flying? Not families going to Fort Lauderdale for $138. The only people in the air will be military, government, and people who were already at the top. Everyone else stays on the ground."



"People wouldn't let that happen."



"They won't have a choice. That's the whole point."



He sat back.



"You asked me what I meant about 2020."



"Yeah."



"2020 proved something. It proved they could shut down your job, your kids' school, your church, your ability to move freely — all of it — and people would go along with it. Because they called it safety. Stay home. Wear this. Please comply."



"Okay."



"What's coming is the same playbook applied to energy. Same language. Same structure. Reduce your usage. Limit your travel. Accept the rolling blackouts. It's for the greater good. Please comply."



"Energy lockdown."



I don't know why I said it like that. It just came out. Like my brain connected it before I gave it permission.



He looked at me. Almost surprised.



"That's the phrase. Watch for it. You'll hear it on the news inside of six weeks."



"And it'll be like 2020."



"Worse. 2020 you could argue about it at a school board meeting. You could refuse to wear the mask. You could keep your business open and take the fine. There was a debate. Two sides."



"And this one?"



"This one isn't a debate. You can't argue with an empty gas pump. You can't protest a grid that shut off. This is the DMV. You show up. You wait. You take what they give you. You comply because there's nothing else to do."



"So why isn't everyone panicking right now?"



He almost smiled. That same tired smile.



"Because they learned. In 2020 they told everyone to panic and everyone did. Bought all the toilet paper. Rushed the stores. Made the whole thing ten times worse. They learned from that. So this time they're doing the opposite. Telling everyone to stay calm. Gas prices will come back down. The peace talks will work. The strait will open. Go about your day."



"But—"



"But look at what they're DOING. They're rationing fuel in a dozen countries. They're shutting down airlines. They're dimming streetlights. They're closing businesses early. They're putting people on four-day weeks because they can't afford five. What they're saying and what they're doing are two completely different things. That's what keeping a lid on it looks like."



He checked his phone again.



"The small dominoes fall first. Cuba. Sri Lanka. Bangladesh. Philippines. Nobody here pays attention to those. Then the medium ones start cracking. Europe. Australia. Asia. That's what you're watching right now — flights cancelled, fuel doubled, brownout advisories going from 'voluntary' to 'required.' That's the middle falling."



"And then us."



"And then us. But by the time the average person in this country looks up and realizes what's happening — the shelves are empty. The gas is gone. The power's going in and out. And the one thing nobody thought about is the one thing that matters most."



"What's that."



"Water."



"Water?"



"When the grid starts going down — and it will, because the same fuel that flies these planes generates your electricity — what do you think happens to your tap?"



I didn't answer.



"Your water doesn't come from a well. It comes from a treatment plant. That plant runs on electricity. The pumps that push the water from that plant to your house run on electricity. The pressure that keeps it flowing through the pipes — electricity."



"Okay."



"When the power goes, the pumps stop. When the pumps stop, the pressure drops. When the pressure drops, your tap goes dry. Not in a week. Not in a day. Hours."



"Hours?"



"Texas. 2021. Three-day grid failure. Fifteen million people lost safe drinking water. Boil advisories for weeks. People were melting snow in their bathtubs. And that was a winter storm. An accident. Nobody planned it."



"Fifteen million."



"From a three-day accident."



He paused.



"Cuba. Right now. Their grid has collapsed completely — four times since last October. The whole island goes dark. Ten million people. Eighteen hours a day without power. And you know what happened the second the power went out? The water stopped. Same chain. No fuel. No power. No pumps. No water. A woman on the news last week — two little kids — said if the electricity doesn't come back by morning she won't be able to get water for her family. Not can't afford it. Can't GET it. The pumps are dead."



"That's been going on for months. That's what it looks like when the fuel runs out and nobody comes to fix it."



He looked at me.



"Now think about what happens when it's not Cuba. When it's not some island the news barely covers. When it's Charlotte. Or Atlanta. Or Phoenix. Same chain. Same result. The only difference is the number of people standing in their kitchen staring at a faucet that doesn't work."



I sat there staring at the empty gate. The Spirit logo on the wall. The red departure board. The janitor sweeping up around us like nothing was different about today.



"Everyone's looking at gas prices right now. Everyone's talking about flights and the strait and oil. Nobody — nobody — is talking about the water. And the people in the rooms I've been sitting in? They know. They've known for months. But the second the public understands what happens to the water when the grid goes down, the shelves go empty in hours. You keep people focused on the thing that scares them a little so they don't find the thing that should scare them a lot."



"So what do you do about it."



"About what."



"The water. If the tap goes dry and the stores are empty and there's no truck coming — what do you actually do."



He looked at his leather case. Looked at his phone. Looked back at me.



"I've got it handled."



"What does that mean."



"It means my family is taken care of."



"Okay, but how."



He shook his head. "I don't talk about this."



"You just spent twenty minutes telling me my kids might not have water. You can't say that and then tell me you've got it handled and leave it there."

You can survive eating rice and canned soup for a month. Your body shuts down in three days without water. Three days. There's no backup plan for that. There's no toughing it out."

He said it flat. Not rude. Just final.

I patted my pockets. Pulled out a crumpled boarding pass — the Spirit ticket to Fort Lauderdale that was never going to get me anywhere. I handed it to him.

When enough people figure out the connection between the grid and the water, the demand is going to be more than any small company can handle. I don't know what the timeline is on that. ## Ad 6 — EMP / Grid Failure Scenario

**Hook:** "Picture this: It's 2:47 AM when the first transformer explodes."
**POV:** 2ème personne ("you") — pas de personnage narrateur défini
**Persona insider:** Le voisin retraité Army (rôle très court, juste "Grid's down. This could be bad.")
**Trigger:** Scénario hypothétique EMP → grid collapse total
**Framework DIFFÉRENT des autres :** Pas de vraie narration insider. C'est un "scénario + éducation + preuve factuelle" — plus proche d'un article de blog survivaliste que d'un native ad narratif.
**Structure :**
1. Scénario immersif 2ème personne (EMP, nuit, chaos)
2. Timeline 72h détaillée (Heure 1-6, 6-12, 12-24, Jour 2, Jour 3)
3. Preuves factuelles (Texas 2021 = 14M, Northeast 2003 = 55M, California 2020)
4. Calcul personnel ("combien d'eau avez-vous MAINTENANT?")
5. Le dilemme : déshydratation 3 jours vs eau contaminée
6. Solution = filtre
7. Pas de progression sociale (pas de femme, frère, etc.)
8. Ton : informatif/éducatif, pas émotionnel

**Différences majeures vs tous les autres scripts :**
- PAS de figure d'insider développée
- PAS de slow burn narratif
- PAS de dialogue étendu
- Format plus court, plus direct
- 2ème personne (YOU) au lieu de 1ère personne (JE)
- Plus "article éducatif" que "histoire vraie"
- Pas de méta-narratif (pas de "ils nous cachent quelque chose")
- Le produit est présenté comme une découverte personnelle du narrateur, pas révélé par un insider

**Script complet :**

Picture this: It's 2:47 AM when the first transformer explodes.



You're jolted awake by the sound — like thunder, but closer. Through your window, you see a flash of blue-white light on the horizon. Then another. And another.



Your phone's dead. You reach for it instinctively, press the power button. Nothing. You try the light switch. Nothing.



You walk to the window and look outside. Your entire neighborhood is dark. No streetlights. No porch lights glowing. Not a single house light anywhere. Even the intersection at the end of your street — the one that always has that annoying blinking yellow light — is completely black.



In the distance, you hear sirens. Lots of them. Then you hear something else. Car alarms. Dozens of them going off at once, then dying out one by one.
